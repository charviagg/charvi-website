*On agricultural waste, entrepreneurial curiosity, and the rather complicated economics of a seemingly simple problem.*

Every year, Punjab produces approximately 20 million tonnes of paddy straw. The Government of India's 2023–24 Crop Residue Management guidelines estimated that around 7.3 million tonnes were being burnt in the state.

<FieldStudyScale />

Come October and November, as the paddy fields make way for wheat, parts of northern India acquire an altogether different complexion. The skies turn a rather unflattering shade of grey, and the agricultural calendar begins to make its presence felt in the air we breathe. Research published in 2024 found that biomass burning could account for as much as 37 per cent of Delhi's daily fine particulate pollution during peak burning periods.

There has, admittedly, been considerable progress in reducing recorded farm fires over recent years. Nevertheless, the underlying economics of *parali*, as we colloquially call paddy residue, caught my curiosity.

<FieldStudyFireChart />

Here was an agricultural by-product that was environmentally damaging when burnt, yet commercially useful when processed. Surely, there was a business somewhere in between?

I began exploring the possibility of creating a digital platform that could connect farmers with businesses looking to procure agricultural residue. The premise was rather straightforward: bridge the information asymmetry between those who had something they wanted to get rid of and those who could put it to productive use.

On paper, it was quite a charming little proposition.

The demand side was particularly interesting. Paddy straw can be converted into compressed biogas through biological decomposition, with applications in transportation, energy and the production of organic fertilisers. India's government has actively encouraged this transition through financial incentives, machinery subsidies and initiatives such as SATAT and the National Bioenergy Programme.

In 2022, a compressed biogas plant in Sangrur was inaugurated with an investment of approximately ₹220 crore, designed to consume around 100,000 tonnes of paddy straw annually.

There was, evidently, value in what was going up in smoke.

For a smaller entrepreneur, however, entering the processing industry was an altogether different proposition. Between the investment in machinery, land, storage, processing infrastructure and working capital, the financial barriers could be considerable. A subsidy might make the economics more favourable, but it did not necessarily make the business accessible.

I was consequently more interested in the intermediary space — the rather less glamorous business of getting straw from Point A to Point B.

And this was where my conversations with farmers began to complicate things.

The agricultural landscape, when reduced to a spreadsheet, appeared to offer an abundance of raw material. On the ground, that abundance was dispersed across numerous landholdings, several of them relatively small.

For a farmer cultivating a few acres, the economics of independently arranging machinery, collecting straw and transporting it to a processing facility were not always favourable. Paddy straw is bulky, relatively low in value per unit of volume, and inconveniently seasonal. Moving it is hardly as effortless as moving a pin on a digital map.

A study by the Council on Energy, Environment and Water estimated that transporting baled straw over distances exceeding 50 kilometres could become commercially unviable. Even at that distance, the estimated delivered cost in 2020 was between ₹2,500 and ₹3,000 per tonne.

Compounding this was the particularly unforgiving agricultural calendar. Farmers typically have a window of just 20 to 25 days between harvesting paddy and preparing their fields for wheat.

For them, the cost of waiting was not merely an inconvenience. It could have implications for the next harvest and, consequently, their livelihood.

What I had initially approached as an environmental problem was revealing itself to be a question of logistics, opportunity cost and conflicting economic incentives.

I also discovered, through some of these conversations, that informal aggregation mechanisms had already emerged. Smaller farmers would sometimes sell or hand over their residue to larger farmers or local aggregators, who possessed better access to machinery, transportation and existing buyers.

In effect, parts of the supply chain I had imagined creating already existed, albeit without the convenience of an app or the polish of a business presentation.

This considerably altered my understanding of the addressable market. Twenty million tonnes of straw generated did not translate into twenty million tonnes available for procurement. Some was incorporated into the soil, some was committed to existing buyers, and some was simply too expensive or inconvenient to collect.

The interesting question, then, was not how much *parali* Punjab produced, but how much could actually be acquired, at what price, within what distance, and in what period of time.

My conversations had taken me from the rather appealing economics of a circular economy to its decidedly less romantic operational realities.

I found the experience particularly valuable because it challenged a tendency I suspect many budding entrepreneurs share: the inclination to see inefficiency and immediately imagine an enterprise in its place.

Sometimes, an apparent inefficiency is the consequence of several perfectly rational decisions made by individuals operating under very different constraints.

The farmer wants his field cleared before the next sowing season. The aggregator needs sufficient volume to justify his transportation costs. The processing company requires a dependable supply throughout the year. The government wants cleaner air. And the entrepreneur, rather optimistically, wants all four interests to converge.

I am still exploring whether there is a meaningful intervention to be made within this ecosystem, particularly in the coordination of collection, aggregation and procurement.

For now, though, I find myself spending rather more time understanding the fields than designing the interface.

Perhaps an appropriate place to begin when one's business idea is, quite literally, rooted in the ground.
