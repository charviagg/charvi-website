*On discovering finance in classrooms, conversations, and the markets.*

For a long time, finance existed in my mind as something rather abstract. I associated it with numbers, spreadsheets, stock prices, and perhaps, vaguely, sales. It was a world I understood in fragments, but not yet in substance.

That changed when I began specialising in Banking and Finance at university.

One of my earliest encounters with the discipline beyond its conventional definitions came in a Sustainable Finance class. Our professor challenged us to construct an investment portfolio from scratch, with two objectives that initially seemed difficult to reconcile: strong financial performance and a high degree of sustainability.

It was my first introduction to the complexity of investment decisions. Returns were no longer the only consideration. We had to think about risk, diversification, environmental and social factors, and the assumptions embedded in our choices. More importantly, I began to understand that finance was not simply about identifying the most profitable asset, but about deciding what constituted value in the first place.

The questions became more interesting as I ventured further into the subject.

During my exchange semester at IE University in Madrid, I took a Venture Capital class taught by a professor whose own life had been shaped by investing. He spoke candidly about his investment successes, but also about the personal spending decisions that eventually brought him back to university as a professor in his thirties.

His story stayed with me because it revealed a distinction I had rarely considered: being skilled at generating wealth is not necessarily the same as being skilled at managing it.

Suddenly, the principles of capital allocation, risk appetite, and financial discipline extended far beyond firms and investment funds. They were equally relevant to individuals, whose decisions were shaped not only by rational calculations but also by ambition, habits, and circumstance.

Finance, I was beginning to realise, was deeply human.

That understanding became more tangible during my four-month internship at **Helios Capital Management**, where I had the opportunity to learn directly from **Mr. Samir Arora**.

One of my earliest memories is of the library in his office. He invited me to choose a book, an invitation that quietly became one of the most formative parts of my internship.

Over the following four months, I read seven or eight books. Most mornings, he would sit me down and ask what I had taken away from my latest reading. What began as a discussion about a chapter or an investment principle would often unfold into something much broader: an experience with a client, the implications of a regulation, or a reflection on building a fund from modest beginnings into an international investment business.

It was an unusual kind of education, one in which the distance between theory and practice grew smaller with every conversation.

Concepts I had encountered in lectures began to acquire texture. A stock price, for instance, was no longer merely a number moving across a screen. It reflected expectations about a company's future cash flows, the risks investors were willing to bear, and the assumptions they made about growth. Valuation became less about arriving at a precise figure and more about understanding why two thoughtful investors could arrive at different conclusions about the same business.

I also began to appreciate the distinction between *price* and *value*. Markets continuously reveal what investors are willing to pay, but that does not necessarily settle what an asset is worth. The difference demands research, conviction, and, perhaps most importantly, intellectual humility.

And behind every investment decision lay another layer: the responsibility of managing someone else's capital. Portfolio construction, liquidity, regulation, and client expectations were not isolated topics from separate university modules. They were interconnected considerations in decisions made under uncertainty.

Looking back, what I valued most about the internship was not simply the exposure to markets. It was the opportunity to observe how experience shapes judgment: how years of investing, navigating changing market conditions, and working with clients can inform a perspective that no textbook can fully reproduce.

University had given me the language to understand finance. My professors had shown me its breadth, from sustainability to venture capital, and from institutional investing to personal financial choices. Helios helped me begin connecting that language to the realities of markets and the people operating within them.

I entered the discipline thinking finance was primarily about numbers.

I left these experiences understanding that the numbers are often where the questions begin.
