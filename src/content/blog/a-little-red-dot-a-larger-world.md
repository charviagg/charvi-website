*On growing up, growing roots, and the education that happened between classrooms.*

Singapore was never quite part of the plan.

I was thriving at Welham Girls' School, a boarding school in India, when an offer for a full-ride scholarship to Global Indian International School in Singapore came my way. It was an opportunity to spend the final two years of my high school in a country I barely knew, and, rather inconveniently, in the middle of a global pandemic.

Having been a boarding school girl, I was well acquainted with the art of packing one's life into a suitcase. This time, however, the distance felt different. Home was on the other side of immigration, and the world was temporarily rather reluctant to let people cross borders.

And so began my little Singaporean chapter, which, unbeknownst to me, would turn into several more.

I took quite a liking to Singapore. Beyond its manicured streets and almost amusing punctuality, there was something invigorating about being surrounded by bright, industrious minds. I was fascinated by the seriousness with which people pursued their interests, and the quiet ambition that seemed to permeate everyday life.

Two years, as it turned out, were hardly enough.

When the time came to choose a university, I decided to stay and pursue my undergraduate degree at Nanyang Technological University. Part of me was curious about how much more there was to discover in this little red dot.

I had become particularly fascinated by the nuances of Singaporean identity. People I met traced their roots to different corners of the world, carried different traditions into their homes, and yet shared a certain unmistakable Singaporeanness. Culture here seemed less like a neatly labelled collection of inherited customs and more like a living, breathing conversation between them.

I wanted to partake in as much of that conversation as possible.

At NTU, my interests took me in wonderfully different directions. I dabbled in hall activities and business clubs, spent time volunteering with elderly residents through the Welfare Services Club, and somehow found myself becoming the President of the NTU Ballroom Dance Club. From business discussions to ballroom floors, my university experience had rather eclectic footwork.

What I loved most was the sheer variety of people these spaces brought into my life. Intellect presented itself in so many different forms. There was much to admire in the way someone approached a business problem, but equally in the patience with which another listened to an elderly stranger, or the discipline with which someone perfected a dance routine.

I found my people somewhere amidst all of this.

Some of my most cherished friendships began in ordinary classrooms. My professors, too, became an important part of my university experience. I grew particularly fond of office hours, where a question about a lecture could quite easily wander into a conversation about careers, ideas, or life itself. There was something lovely about getting to know the minds behind the lecture slides.

Then there were the countless coffee chats with professionals. Somewhere between the coffee and the conversation, I began to develop an appreciation for the commitment people brought to their work in Singapore. I was curious about the professional culture, the attention to detail, and the sense of ownership with which many approached their responsibilities.

By graduation, I had spent years understanding Singapore through its educational institutions, friendships, and communities. I wanted to see it through yet another lens: its workplace.

And what better place to begin than Singtel, a homegrown company whose history is so closely intertwined with Singapore's own development?

My year in Corporate Transformation at Singtel gave me perhaps my most intimate understanding of Singapore yet. Working within an organisation so deeply woven into the country's fabric offered a perspective that my years as a student could not quite have afforded me. I began to appreciate the work, deliberation, and people behind some of the systems I had grown accustomed to as a resident.

There is something rather special about getting to know a country at different stages of one's own life. I had experienced Singapore as a schoolgirl, a university student, and eventually, a working professional. Each version of me inhabited the same little island, yet discovered a slightly different one.

I sometimes wonder when exactly Singapore began to feel like home. There was no particular epiphany, no grand moment of arrival. Perhaps it happened gradually, through familiar faces in lecture theatres, conversations that outlasted their allotted office hours, friendships, dance rehearsals, and one too many cups of coffee.

For someone who had spent much of her formative years living out of suitcases, I had, rather unexpectedly, grown roots.

And what a lovely little island to have grown them on.
